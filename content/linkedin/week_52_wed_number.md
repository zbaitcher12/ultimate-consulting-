Three numbers to put against the five-year bet.

The five-year cumulative return at institutions that made putting the operating model on paper an explicit strategic bet: $3M–$10M in reduced audit remediation, faster procurement cycles, higher staff retention, lower borrowing costs, and improved working capital — none of which appears on any single-year budget line, but all of which show up in the five-year retrospective.

The annual investment required to run the bet: $150K–$400K in dedicated capacity across the five years — a fraction of the returns the bet produces.

The "did this work" recognition timing: year three at the earliest — because the compounding requires the portfolio depth of years one and two to be visible against the audit and cash outcomes of years three, four, and five.

None of these are theoretical. I've watched all three at real institutions.

The investment required to name the bet, assign the executive sponsor, and commit to the year-one foundation work: a fraction of any one of those numbers.

Long-horizon framing is what the record of how the work runs needs, because the returns compound rather than arriving in a single year. The bet that has no constituency is the bet with the highest strategic return.

The presidents who made it — and stayed long enough to see year three — didn't need to be persuaded a second time. Runs when people leave. Holds up when someone asks "says who?"
