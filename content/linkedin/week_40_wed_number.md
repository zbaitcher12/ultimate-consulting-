Three numbers to put against the board scorecard.

The annual cost of the operating-discipline incidents that reach the board only when they've already surfaced: $500K–$2M in audit findings, personnel matters, system failures, and compliance issues — each one a preventable event leadership was tracking too late to intervene.

The board-attention concentration at institutions where the topic appears only as incident report: 100% reactive — meaning every board conversation about the record of how the work runs is a conversation about a problem your institution is already paying for.

The lift in strategic-conversation quality when three quarterly indicators — coverage, currency, handoff documentation — are on the standing scorecard: the board asks about the record before the incident, when the intervention is still cheap.

None of these are theoretical. I've watched all three at real institutions.

The investment required to produce the three-indicator scorecard quarterly — with the trend line over four quarters as the primary view: a fraction of any one of those numbers.

Board reporting determines what the board pays attention to. When the record is a standing scorecard item, leadership manages it. When it appears only in incident reports, leadership responds to it.

Boards measure what matters. The record of how the work runs matters. Runs when people leave. Holds up when someone asks "says who?"

Where would those numbers land at your institution?
