Three numbers to put against the next documentation commission.

The sunk cost of a project that produced a binder nobody uses: $60K–$180K in outside fees, plus the six weeks of internal staff time the discovery interviews absorbed — for a finished document that decays into decoration within twelve months.

The staff time required to reconstruct a lost workflow when the documented version turned out to describe the idealized happy path rather than what staff actually did: 40 to 120 staff hours per workflow, per successor.

The productivity gap for a new hire onboarded against documentation that didn't match operational reality: 60 to 120 days of sidebar coaching before the new hire can run the workflow without asking — extended from the 30 to 60 days they'd otherwise need.

None of these are theoretical. I've watched all three at real institutions.

The investment required to commission work built around the six markers of quality: a fraction of any one of those numbers.

Auditors don't test the binder. They test the workflow — and they read the document against what staff actually do. Documentation that names the happy path and excludes the exceptions is the documentation that produces the finding, because it doesn't describe how your institution actually runs.

If it's not written down the way it actually happens, it doesn't exist. Runs when people leave. Holds up when someone asks "says who?"

Where would those numbers land at your institution?
