Three numbers to put against the CFO dashboard.

The working-capital impact at a mid-size institution whose cash-application aging runs 5 to 10 days longer than peers with documented bursar workflows: $2M–$8M in receivables sitting unposted at any given moment, driving account holds and downstream retention risk.

The annual audit-cost differential at institutions with documented operations versus peers without: $75K–$300K per year in external audit fees, because the audit takes less time when the auditor can trace the evidence to a documented workflow instead of hunting for it.

The annual account-receivable write-off at institutions whose reconciliation and refund workflows aren't on paper at operational depth: $150K–$600K in preventable write-offs your CFO categorizes as bad debt but that traces back to documentation gaps.

None of these are theoretical. I've watched all three at real institutions.

The investment required to fund upstream documentation on the three highest-impact workflows: a fraction of any one of those numbers.

Cash cycle and audit posture are lagging indicators. The record of how the work runs is the leading indicator behind both. The CFO who sees the connection intervenes at the documentation level — where the return compounds.

CFOs who see the connection make the upstream investments. CFOs who don't spend on the symptoms. Runs when people leave. Holds up when someone asks "says who?"

Where would those numbers land at your institution?
