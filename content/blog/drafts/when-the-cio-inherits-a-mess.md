The ERP war story higher-ed CIOs trade most often goes like this. We went live on the new system. Within a month, your institution discovered that configuration choices made before my arrival were locking in workflows that did not match how operations actually ran. The previous CIO had left. The implementation partner had moved on. The decisions were not reversible without significant rework.

---

## This pattern is the modal case, not the exception.

CIOs in higher education inherit operating models that were configured by predecessors who did not document the operating assumptions, by implementation partners who took the requirements document at face value, and by leadership teams who did not require operating-model clarity as a prerequisite for configuration approval.

The staff who could have explained the gap between configured workflow and actual workflow are usually the same staff who were excluded from the configuration discussions.

The pattern is preventable. Not in the moment the new CIO arrives — at that point the configuration is locked — but in the operating cycle that precedes the transition.

---

## The briefing your institution should produce before the transition.

Prevention is a CIO Operational Readiness Briefing: a structured document an incoming CIO can read in their first week and use to make defensible configuration decisions for the next eighteen months.

The briefing covers four areas:

**Documentation maturity.** By functional area, with the highest-risk undocumented workflows named.

**Configuration-to-workflow alignment.** Which configuration decisions match documented workflow and which do not.

**The decision-authority chain.** Load-bearing escalation paths named, with gaps between documented and actual routing flagged.

**Audit-readiness posture.** Highest-risk finding categories named, with remediation work in progress.

---

## Ten to fifteen pages, produced over six weeks.

The briefing is not a long document. Ten to fifteen pages. Executive-summary length per area, with one-page deep-dives on the three highest-risk items in each.

The work to produce it happens in the six months before the CIO transition — not after, when the new CIO is already making decisions without the context. The six-week cycle includes the CIO's direct reports, the chief audit officer, and the cabinet liaison.

---

## A question worth asking now.

Every CIO who has taken over a mid-stream implementation has the same story. The briefing is the document that should have existed.

If a new CIO joined your institution tomorrow, what document would you hand them on day one?

The institutions that produce the briefing before the transition are the institutions that avoid the next iteration of the story. The institutions that don't are the institutions whose next CIO will be telling the same story to peers at the next industry gathering.
