Your institution's memory lives in a few people. The financial aid specialist who knows exactly which override sequence corrects a Pell calculation when SAP runs late. The bursar staff member who keeps cash-application reconciliation moving when an integration drops a batch. The registrar's deputy who is the only person who actually understands the residency-classification logic for five campuses across three counties.

These are not edge cases. They are load-bearing workflows. They produce outcomes the institution depends on. And they exist only in memory.

---

## Invisible until they fail.

Most leadership teams know this is happening. Few have a plan before something forces the question. Federal program reviews force it. Cutover forces it. Staff turnover forces it. The pattern is consistent: the workflow becomes visible at the moment it fails, and the failure is the most expensive way to discover the paper was never there.

Undocumented workflows do not announce themselves. They are invisible to leadership because they are invisible to the staff who execute them. Staff absorbed the workarounds into normal practice years ago and no longer recognize them as workarounds.

---

## Three categories. Three failure modes.

**Tribal knowledge.** The workflow your institution has been running the same way for years without writing it down because everyone who needs it already knows it. Fails on staff turnover. When the carrier leaves, the next person rebuilds the workflow from scratch.

**Configured-around exceptions.** The workarounds staff built because the system did not handle a real-world case. The workarounds produce correct outcomes. They are not documented because they are, technically, not the official process. Fails on system migration. When the system changes, the workaround disappears, and nobody knows what it did.

**Ghost approvals.** The decisions that get made every week without documented authority because the formal authority chain produced a five-day delay and someone short-circuited it. The short-circuit became permanent. Fails on audit. The reviewer asks who authorized the decision. There is no trail.

---

## Pre-modernization is where the risk compounds.

All three categories fail more visibly during pre-modernization than at any other point in the operating cycle. Configuration choices lock in the assumption that documented workflows describe how the work actually runs. They don't. The configuration built on that assumption produces eighteen months of post-go-live cleanup.

The paper is the readiness. Write it before the system does.

---

## A question worth asking now.

The workflows that fail are the workflows nobody knew the institution depended on.

Which of your highest-risk workflows lives only in one staff member's memory — and what happens when that person leaves?

The institutions that ask this question and act on the answer are the institutions that exit the next staff transition without the public consequence. The institutions that defer the question discover the dependency at the moment it becomes a finding.
