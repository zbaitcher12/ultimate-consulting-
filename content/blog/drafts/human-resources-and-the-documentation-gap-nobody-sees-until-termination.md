Human resources workflows are the operational work your institution defers writing down until the moment it's being defended in court. The deferral has a reason. The workflows feel sensitive. Nobody wants to write down a termination protocol while your institution is functioning normally.

The combination produces a gap that becomes visible only when your institution faces an EEOC investigation, a Title IX complaint that goes to litigation, or a wrongful-termination claim.

---

## Four HR workflows that show up consistently underdocumented.

**Onboarding sequence.** The workflow that moves a new hire from offer acceptance through first day, with the I-9 verification, benefits enrollment, system access provisioning, and orientation logistics that fail when any step is skipped.

**Title IX response.** The routing that determines how a Title IX complaint is intake, investigated, and resolved, with the regulatory timing requirements and the documentation standards the regulation now specifies.

**Performance documentation.** The cadence and form of supervisory documentation that establishes performance issues over time, with the progressive-discipline structure that supports defensible termination decisions.

**Termination protocol.** The sequence that moves an employee from termination decision through final paycheck, system deprovisioning, exit interview, and the legal-record retention that survives subsequent claims.

---

## Each failure mode produces legal exposure.

Onboarding gaps produce I-9 violations that produce ICE penalties. Title IX gaps produce regulatory findings and private litigation. Performance documentation gaps produce wrongful-termination claims that succeed because the institutional record does not support the termination decision. Termination protocol gaps produce final-paycheck violations, system-access risk, and post-employment claims.

The SOPs that close these gaps are not policy statements. They are operational workflows that name the steps, the timing, the actors, the documentation requirements, and the escalation routing.

---

## Why the return compounds across institutional scales.

The case matters at every scale. At larger institutions, the volume of HR actions produces regular opportunities for defensible records to be the difference between a routine matter and a protracted one. At institutions with more concentrated operations, each individual action sits closer to the operating cycle, which means the defensible record translates directly into institutional throughput.

HR documentation is uncomfortable work. The institutions that do it produce defensible records when they need them. The institutions that defer it discover, in litigation, that the absence of documentation is more dispositive than the presence of imperfect documentation.

## A question worth asking now.

Which HR SOP at your institution would hold up in an EEOC investigation — and which wouldn't?
